### 読解パッセージ

The announcement that a fresh heatwave is forecast to push temperatures as high as 34 degrees Celsius, prompting authorities to issue a week-long health alert, illustrates how quickly extreme heat has become a recurring feature of the British summer rather than an isolated anomaly. According to meteorologist Stav Danaos, this episode is expected to persist considerably longer than the heatwave that preceded it earlier in the season, leaving health officials little time to stand down emergency protocols before reactivating them. It is the extended duration of the alert, rather than the peak temperature alone, that has drawn particular attention from public health specialists monitoring the nation's response.

Researchers who study heat-related illness emphasize that it is sustained exposure, not a single scorching afternoon, that poses the greatest danger to human health. When nighttime temperatures fail to fall much below the daytime peak—so-called tropical nights—the body loses its usual opportunity to recover, placing additional strain on the cardiovascular and respiratory systems. This effect compounds day after day during a prolonged heatwave, which helps explain why previous extended hot spells in the United Kingdom have coincided with measurable increases in mortality among older adults and people with pre-existing conditions, even when the maximum temperature recorded was no higher than in shorter episodes.

Some climate scientists link the growing frequency of such prolonged hot spells to shifts in the jet stream associated with a warming atmosphere, though many are careful to note that no single heatwave can be attributed entirely to climate change; rather, warming is understood to make episodes of this kind more likely and more intense over time. This distinction matters for policy, since it raises the question of whether Britain's housing stock and hospitals, both largely designed with cold winters rather than hot summers in mind, are adequately prepared for a period in which extended heat alerts are becoming routine rather than exceptional.

The author contends that repeated short-term warnings, however well communicated, cannot substitute for structural adaptation. Alerts urging people to stay hydrated and avoid outdoor exertion address immediate risk, but they do little to remedy the underlying vulnerability created by poorly insulated homes, a scarcity of shaded public space, and hospitals without adequate cooling. Investment in measures such as reflective building materials, urban greening, and cooling centers would, the author argues, do more over the long run to protect public health than any single week-long advisory, however necessary that advisory may be in the short term.
